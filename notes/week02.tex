\section{The Six Trigonometric Functions}
\noindent The six trigonometric functions are defined by coordinates on the unit circle and ratios of those coordinates. Their relationships determine their signs, domains, and graphs.


\medskip
Let $P=(x,y)$ be the point at angle $\theta$ on the unit circle. Its coordinates are $x=\cos\theta$ and $y=\sin\theta$. The remaining four functions are formed from quotients and reciprocals of these coordinates.

\noindent\textbf{Learning objectives.} Define the six functions on the unit circle, calculate exact values in each quadrant, determine their domains, identify characteristic graph features, and apply trigonometric ratios to measurement problems.


\subsection{From a point to six functions}
Last week we studied the unit-circle point $P=(x,y)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. The two coordinates determine four additional ratios whenever the denominators are nonzero:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sin\theta&=y,& \cos\theta&=x,&
\tan\theta&=\frac{y}{x},\\
\csc\theta&=\frac1y,& \sec\theta&=\frac1x,&
\cot\theta&=\frac{x}{y}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the six functions encode related properties of a single point.

\begin{definition}The \emph{tangent} function is $\tan\theta=\sin\theta/\cos\theta$ where $\cos\theta\neq0$. The functions $\csc,\sec,\cot$ are the reciprocals of $\sin,\cos,\tan$ respectively, wherever defined.\end{definition}

\begin{example}At $\theta=\pi/3$ the circle point is $(1/2,\sqrt3/2)$. Thus $\tan\theta=\sqrt3$, $\sec\theta=2$, $\csc\theta=2/\sqrt3=2\sqrt3/3$, and $\cot\theta=1/\sqrt3=\sqrt3/3$.\end{example}

\subsection{Tangent as a slope}
For $\cos\theta\ne0$, the line through the origin and $P=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ has slope
\[
m=\frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}
=\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}=\tan\theta.
\]
Tangent therefore gives the slope of the terminal line. Its magnitude increases without bound as the line approaches a vertical position, at which the slope is undefined.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.7]

\draw[->,gray] (0,-.35)--(0,1.55) node[above]{$y$};
\draw[thick,courseblue] (0,0) circle (1);
\draw[very thick,coursegold] (0,0)--(35:1.5);
\draw[dashed,coursegreen] (35:1)--({cos(35)},0);
\draw[thick,coursegreen] (1,0)--(1,{tan(35)});
\draw[dashed] (1,-.28)--(1,1.3);
\fill[courseblue] (35:1) circle (.025);
\node[right] at (1,{tan(35)/2}) {\scriptsize $\tan\theta$};
\node[below] at (.6,0) {\scriptsize $\cos\theta$};
\node[left] at ({cos(35)},{sin(35)/2}) {\scriptsize $\sin\theta$};
\node at (.25,.12) {$\theta$};
\draw[->] (.22,0) arc(0:35:.22);
\node[above right] at (1,1.25) {\scriptsize tangent line $x=1$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

The terminal line intersects $x=1$ at $(1,\tan\theta)$, whenever $\cos\theta\ne0$. Hence tangent also has a geometric interpretation as a directed length on a line tangent to the unit circle.

\begin{example}
At $30^\circ$, the unit-circle coordinates are
$(\sqrt3/2,1/2)$. The slope of the corresponding ray is
\[
\tan30^\circ=\frac{1/2}{\sqrt3/2}=\frac{\sqrt3}{3}.
\]
This is also the slope of a line rising one unit for every $\sqrt3$ units of horizontal displacement.
\end{example}


\subsection{Signs and the quadrants}
The coordinate signs determine every trig-function sign. In quadrant I both coordinates are positive; in II, only sine is positive; in III, tangent is positive because both coordinates are negative; and in IV, only cosine is positive. Reciprocal functions have the same signs as their corresponding original functions.

\begin{example}For $\theta=5\pi/4$, the reference angle is $\pi/4$ and $P=(-\sqrt2/2,-\sqrt2/2)$. Therefore $\tan\theta=1$, $\sec\theta=-\sqrt2$, and $\csc\theta=-\sqrt2$.\end{example}


\medskip
For a useful sign check, consider $150^\circ=5\pi/6$. The point is
$(-\sqrt3/2,1/2)$. Both sine and cosecant are positive, both cosine
and secant are negative, and tangent and cotangent are negative:
\[
\tan(5\pi/6)=-\frac{\sqrt3}{3},\qquad
\sec(5\pi/6)=-\frac{2\sqrt3}{3},\qquad
\csc(5\pi/6)=2.
\]
The signs of the six functions follow directly from the signs of sine and cosine.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|ccc|ccc}
& $\sin$ & $\cos$ & $\tan$ & $\csc$ & $\sec$ & $\cot$\\\hline
Quadrant I & $+$ & $+$ & $+$ & $+$ & $+$ & $+$\\
Quadrant II & $+$ & $-$ & $-$ & $+$ & $-$ & $-$\\
Quadrant III & $-$ & $-$ & $+$ & $-$ & $-$ & $+$\\
Quadrant IV & $-$ & $+$ & $-$ & $-$ & $+$ & $-$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{practice}
The terminal point of an angle lies in quadrant IV and satisfies
$\cos\theta=3/5$. Determine $\sin\theta$, $\tan\theta$,
and their reciprocals, showing how the quadrant tells you the signs.
\end{practice}


\subsection{Domains and undefined values}
Division by zero is undefined. In particular, $\tan\theta$ and $\sec\theta$ do not exist where $\cos\theta=0$, at $\pi/2+k\pi$. Similarly $\cot\theta$ and $\csc\theta$ do not exist where $\sin\theta=0$, at $k\pi$, for any integer $k$. Undefined values must be distinguished from zeros of a function.

\begin{practice}Find all six trig-function values at $\theta=3\pi/2$, identifying every undefined value.\end{practice}


\subsection{Zeros and vertical asymptotes}
Near zeros of their denominators, trigonometric quotients may become arbitrarily large in magnitude. This behavior accounts for their vertical asymptotes.

For example, as $\theta$ approaches $\pi/2$ from the left,
$\cos\theta$ approaches zero from above. Consequently
$\sec\theta=1/\cos\theta$ increases without bound. From the right,
cosine is slightly negative, and secant becomes a large negative
number. At $\pi/2$ itself, secant has no value.

The line $\theta=\pi/2$ is a vertical asymptote of both tangent and secant.

\subsection{Right-triangle ratios}
For an acute angle $\theta$ in a right triangle, scale the triangle so its hypotenuse has length $1$. The opposite and adjacent sides then give the coordinates $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$. Restoring a general hypotenuse $h$ gives
\[
\sin\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{h},\quad
\cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{h},\quad
\tan\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}.
\]
The unit-circle definitions extend the right-triangle ratios to arbitrary real angles.

\begin{example}A ramp rises $1.5$ meters over a horizontal run of $6$ meters. Its angle of inclination satisfies $\tan\theta=1.5/6=1/4$. Therefore $\theta=\arctan(1/4)\approx14.0^\circ$.\end{example}


\medskip
Writing $o$, $a$, and $h$ for opposite side, adjacent side, and hypotenuse, respectively, gives
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
&\text{opposite and hypotenuse}&
\text{adjacent and hypotenuse}&\text{opposite and adjacent}\\\hline
\text{direct}&\sin\theta=o/h&\cos\theta=a/h&\tan\theta=o/a\\
\text{reciprocal}&\csc\theta=h/o&\sec\theta=h/a&\cot\theta=a/o
\end{array}
\]
The six ratios describe the same triangle and agree with the unit-circle definitions for acute angles.

\begin{example}
A support cable stretches $13$ meters from the ground to a point
$12$ meters above it. The horizontal run is
$\sqrt{13^2-12^2}=5$ meters. If $\theta$ is the angle with the ground,
then
\[
\sin\theta=\frac{12}{13},\quad
\cos\theta=\frac5{13},\quad
\tan\theta=\frac{12}{5},\quad
\csc\theta=\frac{13}{12},\quad
\sec\theta=\frac{13}{5},\quad
\cot\theta=\frac5{12}.
\]
Each quotient expresses a relation between lengths of the same triangle.
\end{example}

\subsection{Graphs of the trigonometric functions}
On a trigonometric graph, the independent variable is an angle and the dependent variable is a coordinate or ratio at that angle. Such a graph is distinct from the circular trajectory of the associated point.

Sine and cosine remain between $-1$ and $1$. Both repeat after a
full rotation, so their fundamental period is $2\pi$ radians,
or $360^\circ$. Tangent and cotangent repeat after half a turn,
so their fundamental period is $\pi$ radians, or $180^\circ$.
Secant and cosecant share the $2\pi$ period of their reciprocal partners.

\begin{center}

\node[below] at (3.14159,0) {\scriptsize $\pi$};

\node[below] at (6.28319,0) {\scriptsize $2\pi$};
\node[courseblue] at (5.5,1.2) {\scriptsize cosine};
\node[coursegreen] at (3.65,-1.18) {\scriptsize sine};
\end{tikzpicture}

The zeros, excluded inputs, and fundamental period determine important features of a trigonometric graph.

\begin{example}
Rotation by $\pi$ changes the signs of both unit-circle coordinates. Thus
\[
\frac{\sin(\theta+\pi)}{\cos(\theta+\pi)}
=\frac{-\sin\theta}{-\cos\theta}
=\tan\theta.
\]
Therefore tangent has period $\pi$ on its domain.
\end{example}

endpoints?
\end{practice}


\subsection{Applications: orientation and measurement}
Suppose an observer stands $40$ m from the foot of a vertical tower. The angle of elevation to its top is $35^\circ$. The tower's height $h$ satisfies
\[
\tan35^\circ=\frac{h}{40},\qquad h=40\tan35^\circ\approx28.0\text{ m}.
\]
Here the tangent function links an angle to a ratio of lengths. Always sketch the physical situation and label which distances are opposite and adjacent before calculating.


\subsection{Measurement with trigonometric ratios}
The appropriate trigonometric ratio depends on the available lengths. Tangent relates height to horizontal distance; sine relates height to line-of-sight distance.

\noindent\textbf{Worked example: height measurement.}
An observer is $20$ meters from the base of a tree and measures an
angle of elevation of $40^\circ$ to its top. If the observer's eyes
are $1.6$ meters above ground, the elevation of the treetop above
eye level is
\[
h_{\text{above eyes}}=20\tan40^\circ\approx16.8\text{ m}.
\]
The tree height is therefore about $16.8+1.6=18.4$ meters.
The observer's eye height must be added to determine the height above ground.

\begin{practice}
A rescue drone is observed at an elevation angle of $25^\circ$
from an observer whose eye level is $1.7$ meters above ground.
The point directly beneath the drone is $90$ meters away
horizontally. Estimate the drone's altitude.
\end{practice}

\begin{enumerate}
\item At $\theta=2\pi/3$, find all six functions exactly.
\item At $\theta=7\pi/4$, find $\tan,\cot,\sec,\csc$.
\item Explain geometrically why $\tan(\pi/2)$ is undefined.
\item For which angles in $[0,2\pi)$ is $\csc\theta$ undefined?
\item A right triangle has opposite side $5$ and adjacent side $12$. Find all six trig ratios exactly.
\item A ladder leans against a wall, reaching $8$ m high. Its foot is $3$ m from the wall. Find the angle with the ground.
\item A drone is viewed at an elevation angle of $28^\circ$ from a point $75$ m horizontally from the point below it. Estimate its altitude.
\item Show that $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$ implies $1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta$ wherever $\cos\theta\neq0$.
\item Find the exact values of all six trig functions at $11\pi/6$.
\item Describe the zeros and vertical asymptotes of $y=\tan x$ on $[-2\pi,2\pi]$.
\item Sketch one period of $y=\sec x$, labeling asymptotes and its lowest positive and highest negative values.
\item Explain why neither $\csc x$ nor $\sec x$ can equal $1/2$.
\item If $\cos\theta=-12/13$ and $\theta$ lies in quadrant II, determine all other trig values exactly.
\item Show using the unit circle that $\tan(\theta+\pi)=\tan\theta$ wherever both sides are defined.
\item A point on a rotating wheel has unit-circle coordinates $(0,-1)$. Identify the angles in $[0,2\pi)$ and all four undefined or zero-valued functions.
\end{enumerate}
