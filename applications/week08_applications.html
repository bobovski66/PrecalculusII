Module 8 Applications

Curves in motion, optics, and navigation

1. Tracking a launched object

A foam ball is launched from a platform 1.5 m high at 18 m/s, angled 40° above the horizontal. Assume gravitational acceleration is 9.8 m/s² and neglect air resistance.

  1. Write parametric equations for the ball's position (x(t),y(t)).
  2. Explain which term causes vertical acceleration.
  3. Eliminate t to obtain a Cartesian equation for the trajectory.
  4. Why does the parametric representation give more information about the ball's movement?

2. An elliptical track

A vehicle follows the closed path x(t)=30cos t, y(t)=20sin t, with x and y measured in meters.

  1. Eliminate the parameter to determine the Cartesian curve.
  2. Find its horizontal and vertical semiaxes.
  3. Describe the point's initial position and its direction of motion as t increases from 0.
  4. What changes if t is replaced by 2t?

3. A parabolic reflector

A small reflector has cross-section x²=12y with x and y in centimeters. Parallel incoming rays are ideally reflected toward the focus of the parabola.

  1. Find the vertex, focus, and directrix.
  2. Explain why knowing the focus is useful when positioning a sensor.
  3. Compare the geometric definition of a parabola to the equation.

4. A hyperbolic difference of distances

Two fixed listening stations are positioned at the foci of a hyperbola. An observed difference in arrival distances may constrain the source to a hyperbolic branch. Consider x²/16−y²/9=1.

  1. Find the vertices, focal distance, and foci.
  2. Find both asymptotes.
  3. Explain why a difference of distances, rather than a sum, naturally produces a hyperbola.

A curve may be a path, a boundary, or a constraint. The mathematical description should match the kind of information you want from the model.
