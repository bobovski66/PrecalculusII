Module 4 Applications

Recovering angles and simplifying geometric models

1. An inaccessible ridge

A field team measures a horizontal distance of 75 m from the base of a rocky ridge. The ridge rises 45 m above the team's instrument. The angle of elevation is not known, but the ratio of opposite to adjacent distances is.

  1. Draw the right triangle and label the sides.
  2. Write an equation for the angle using a trig ratio. Why is an inverse function needed?
  3. Calculate the elevation angle to the nearest tenth of a degree.
  4. Explain the difference between arctan(45/75) and 1/tan(45/75). Are they measuring the same thing?

2. A robot's orientation

A robot's heading is recorded as 150° from the positive eastward axis. Its direction vector is (cos150°,sin150°). A navigation program receives only the vertical coordinate and uses arcsin to reconstruct an angle.

  1. Find the vertical coordinate exactly.
  2. What angle does arcsin report?
  3. Why doesn't that answer recover the original heading? What additional information would distinguish the two headings?
  4. Explain why preserving both coordinate signs resolves the quadrant ambiguity.

3. Two rotations compose

A camera platform turns through 45° and then another 30°. Rather than guessing the final horizontal coordinate, we can compute cosine of the sum using the coordinates of each rotation.

  1. Calculate cos75° exactly using the cosine addition formula.
  2. Calculate sin75° exactly using the sine addition formula.
  3. Verify that the two values satisfy sin²75°+cos²75°=1.
  4. Explain why trigonometric identities are especially useful when combining rotations.

A mathematical identity is stronger than agreement at one angle: it establishes a relationship everywhere both sides are defined.
